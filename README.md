## Article I: Papal Enclave of 1503 - Draft

# The Conclave of Force and Ambition: Giuliano della Rovere Becomes Pope Julius II (1503)

The year 1503 was tumultuous for the papacy and for Rome. The death of Pope Alexander VI (Rodrigo Borgia) on August 18th marked the end of a pontificate infamous for its ambition, nepotism, and secular focus, which had profoundly destabilized the Papal States and Italian politics.[^1] The subsequent months witnessed not one, but two papal conclaves, culminating in the election of one of the most formidable figures ever to occupy the Throne of St. Peter: Giuliano della Rovere, who took the pontifical name Julius II. His election was not merely a change of leadership; it signaled a dramatic shift in papal policy, a rejection of the Borgia legacy, and the dawn of an era defined by artistic grandeur and relentless military campaigning aimed at restoring papal authority.

## Prelude: The Shadow of the Borgia Bull and a Fleeting Pontificate

Alexander VI's reign (1492-1503) cast a long shadow over the Church. His Spanish origins, perceived worldliness, and especially his ruthless advancement of his children – notably Cesare Borgia – engendered widespread fear and resentment among the Italian cardinals and ruling families.[^2] Cesare, Duke of Valentinois and Gonfaloniere of the Church, had carved out a personal duchy in the Romagna region through brutal efficiency, displacing traditional papal vicars and creating a powerful, albeit temporary, independent state within papal territory.[^3] Alexander's death threw Cesare's future, and the stability of central Italy, into immediate question. The cardinals assembling for the first conclave in September 1503 were deeply divided, navigating the complex web of Borgia influence, pressures from foreign powers (France and Spain), and their own ambitions.[^4]

Into this charged atmosphere stepped Giuliano della Rovere, Cardinal-Priest of San Pietro in Vincoli. A nephew of Pope Sixtus IV, della Rovere was a veteran of papal politics, known for his fierce temper, immense ambition, and long-standing, bitter rivalry with Alexander VI, which had forced him into exile for much of the Borgia pontificate.[^5] He represented a potential force for stability and restoration, yet his own history of political maneuvering and suspected ambition made him a divisive figure.

The September conclave, likely seeking a compromise or perhaps fearing the immediate ascendancy of a strong personality like della Rovere, elected the aged and infirm Cardinal Francesco Todeschini Piccolomini, nephew of Pope Pius II. He took the name Pius III.[^6] His pontificate, however, was one of the shortest in history, lasting a mere twenty-six days before his death on October 18th, 1503. His brief reign did little to resolve the underlying tensions or curb the remaining influence of Cesare Borgia, who, though weakened by illness contracted likely alongside his father, still commanded troops and influence near Rome.[^7]

Pius III's death necessitated a second conclave within weeks. This time, della Rovere was determined to secure the papacy. He engaged in swift and decisive maneuvering. Reports suggest extensive promises were made to fellow cardinals, involving benefices, positions, and possibly direct payments – actions that bordered on or constituted simony, the very sin Alexander VI was often accused of.[^8] Critically, della Rovere struck a deal with Cesare Borgia, promising to confirm Cesare's position as Gonfaloniere and support his authority in the Romagna in exchange for the crucial votes of the Spanish cardinals loyal to the Borgias.[^9] This unlikely, pragmatic alliance neutralized a key obstacle.

The conclave that opened on October 31st, 1503, was remarkably brief. Within hours, on November 1st, Giuliano della Rovere secured the necessary votes and was elected Pope, choosing the name Julius II. His choice of name was significant, evoking not a saintly predecessor but Julius Caesar, signaling an intent to rule with temporal power and authority.[^10]

One of Julius II's first acts, demonstrating the dramatic shift from the Borgia era and fulfilling a deep-seated desire, was his effective repudiation of his predecessor. Though he initially made promises to Cesare Borgia to secure the election, Julius quickly turned against him once firmly seated on the papal throne. He demanded the return of key fortresses in the Romagna held by Cesare's forces. When Cesare hesitated, Julius had him arrested and effectively stripped him of his power base in Italy, forcing him to relinquish his holdings before allowing him to leave for Naples (where he was later arrested by Spanish forces).[^9] Julius was determined to reclaim every inch of papal territory Cesare had conquered and erase the independent Borgia power structure. He went further, reportedly refusing to inhabit the Borgia Apartments in the Vatican Palace, famously declaring, "I will not live in the same rooms as the Borgias. He [Alexander VI] desecrated the Holy Church as none before."[^11] This symbolic act underscored his intention to cleanse the papacy of the Borgia stain and restore its authority and perceived dignity.

## Rise and Early Rule: The Warrior Pope and the Master Artist

Julius II ascended the papal throne at the age of 60, possessed of formidable energy and an unshakeable belief in the necessity of a strong, politically independent papacy controlling a unified Papal State. His primary goals were clear: expel foreign powers (particularly the French) from Italy, subdue rebellious papal vassals, centralize administration, and restore the territorial integrity of the lands considered the Patrimony of St. Peter.[^5]

His early rule was characterized by vigorous action. He immediately began efforts to recover territories lost or semi-detached during the Borgia period, demanding allegiance and control from cities like Perugia and Bologna. This political and administrative consolidation was coupled with a grand vision for Rome itself. Julius sought to transform the city into a worthy capital of Christendom, rivaling the glories of the ancient Roman Empire. This ambition led him to become one of the greatest patrons of the arts in papal history.

Central to this artistic renewal was his tumultuous but profoundly fruitful relationship with Michelangelo Buonarroti. Recognizing Michelangelo's genius, Julius commissioned him in 1505 to create a colossal free-standing tomb for himself, intended for placement in the new St. Peter's Basilica, another project Julius initiated under the guidance of architect Donato Bramante.[^12] The tomb project was conceived on an immense scale, featuring dozens of statues, and consumed Michelangelo's energies initially. However, Julius's mercurial temperament, shifting priorities, and possibly the influence of rivals like Bramante led to the project being repeatedly interrupted and downscaled over decades, causing immense frustration for the artist.[^13]

The most famous outcome of their relationship stemmed from one such interruption. In 1508, Julius II effectively ordered Michelangelo, against the artist's own inclination (as he considered himself primarily a sculptor), to paint the ceiling of the Sistine Chapel.[^12] The project was monumental, covering over 500 square meters of curved ceiling. Despite their frequent and legendary clashes – tales abound of the impatient Pope demanding faster progress and the equally stubborn artist insisting on his methods – the four years (1508-1512) Michelangelo spent painting the ceiling resulted in one of the undisputed masterpieces of Western art.[^13] The Pope's demanding patronage pushed Michelangelo to transcend his own perceived limitations, while the artist's genius gave visual form to the spiritual and temporal ambitions of Julius's reign. Alongside Michelangelo and Bramante, Julius also employed Raphael, commissioning him to decorate the papal apartments (the Stanze di Raffaello), further solidifying Rome's position as the vibrant center of the High Renaissance.[^14]

## Martial Achievements: *Il Papa Terribile* and the Italian Wars

Julius II earned the sobriquet *Il Papa Terribile* ("The Fearsome Pope" or "The Terrible Pope") not only for his fearsome temper but for his unprecedented personal involvement in military affairs. Unlike previous popes who directed policy from Rome, Julius often donned armor and personally led his troops on campaign, enduring the hardships of the field alongside them.[^5] This martial approach was driven by his overarching goal: the restoration and expansion of the Papal States and the assertion of papal supremacy in Italian politics.

His initial campaigns focused on rebellious cities within papal territory. In 1506, he marched first on Perugia, forcing the tyrant Giampaolo Baglioni to submit without a fight. He then advanced on Bologna, ruled by the Bentivoglio family under French protection. Despite the risks, Julius entered Bologna triumphantly after the Bentivoglio fled, re-establishing direct papal control.[^15] These successes demonstrated his resolve and significantly boosted papal prestige.

Julius's ambitions inevitably drew him into the complex conflicts known as the Italian Wars, involving major European powers vying for dominance on the peninsula. His primary targets became the Republic of Venice, which had occupied papal cities in the Romagna (like Rimini and Faenza) after Cesare Borgia's fall, and the Kingdom of France, whose presence in Milan and influence over Genoa he viewed as the main obstacle to Italian (and papal) autonomy.

In 1508, Julius became a key architect of the League of Cambrai, an alliance initially uniting the Papacy, the Holy Roman Empire (Maximilian I), France (Louis XII), and Spain (Ferdinand II) *against* Venice.[^16] Papal forces participated alongside the French in the decisive Battle of Agnadello (1509), which crushed Venetian military power on the mainland and allowed Julius to recover the desired cities in the Romagna.

However, having achieved his objectives against Venice, Julius II executed a stunning diplomatic reversal. Fearing the growing power of France in northern Italy more than the weakened Venice, he absolved the Venetians in 1510 and began working to forge an alliance *against* his former French allies. His rallying cry became "*Fuori i barbari!*" ("Out with the barbarians!"), directed squarely at the French.[^10] This led to the formation of the Holy League in 1511, uniting the Papacy, Venice, Spain, England (Henry VIII), and Swiss mercenaries against Louis XII.[^17]

The war of the Holy League saw Julius again actively involved. Papal forces fought alongside their allies across northern Italy. A notable moment of personal papal engagement, often cited, was the Siege of Mirandola in the winter of 1510-1511, where the elderly Pope braved freezing conditions and enemy cannon fire in the trenches to encourage his troops.[^5] The League experienced fluctuating fortunes. The French won a costly victory at the Battle of Ravenna in 1512, where their brilliant general Gaston de Foix was killed. Despite the tactical defeat for the League, the French losses (especially de Foix) were severe enough that, combined with Swiss pressure, they were forced to withdraw from Milan later that year.[^17] Julius could claim a strategic success, having driven the French temporarily out of Lombardy.

Regarding wider geopolitical ambitions, while Julius's overwhelming focus was the Italian peninsula and the consolidation of the Papal States, some contemporary accounts and later histories suggest he entertained broader, if perhaps less developed, geopolitical ideas. There is discussion among historians regarding whether Julius II gave serious consideration to organizing a crusade aimed at reclaiming Constantinople, which had fallen to the Ottoman Turks in 1453.[^18] Such ambitions were not uncommon rhetoric for Renaissance popes, harkening back to earlier crusading ideals, though often subordinate to more immediate political realities. Evidence for concrete planning by Julius for a Constantinopolitan venture remains elusive, overshadowed by the pressing Italian Wars. Similarly, while cooperation with Balkan Christian forces against the Ottomans was a recurring theme in papal diplomacy throughout the 15th and 16th centuries (building on the legacy of figures like Skanderbeg in Albania), specific documented instances of major military collaboration initiated by Julius II with Orthodox powers like the Serbs or Wallachians, beyond general diplomatic overtures or financial support common for the era, are not well-attested in mainstream accounts of his pontificate.[^19] His energies were primarily consumed by consolidating power closer to Rome. Any grand Eastern design likely remained a secondary, perhaps aspirational, objective compared to the urgent task of securing the Papal States.[^20]

## Conclusion: A Legacy of Strength and Splendor

Pope Julius II died on February 21, 1513. He left behind a papacy politically stronger and territorially more secure than it had been for generations. His relentless military campaigns had succeeded in restoring papal authority over central Italy, expelling (albeit temporarily) the French, and establishing the Pope as a major temporal power broker on the European stage.[^10] His legacy, however, is complex. The "Warrior Pope" secured the Church's earthly power but did so through means – warfare, intricate diplomacy, ruthless ambition – that seemed to subordinate spiritual concerns to political ones, contributing to criticisms that would later fuel the Protestant Reformation.[^2]

Simultaneously, his vision and patronage transformed Rome, fostering the genius of Michelangelo, Raphael, and Bramante, and leaving an indelible mark on Western art and architecture.[^14] The 1503 conclave that elected him proved to be a pivotal moment. It brought an end to the blatant excesses of the Borgia era and installed a leader whose *terribilità* reshaped the papacy's political fortunes and the physical landscape of its capital. Julius II remains a figure of contradictions – a spiritual leader clad in armor, a cultivator of sublime beauty through sheer force of will – but undeniably one of the most consequential popes of the Renaissance.
